# Narrative writing

The subject can be an idea, an experience, or the person. Read the current
source and project guidance. They establish facts, the author's perspective,
accepted wording and edit boundaries. Use writing-style for sentence craft.
The author's samples establish voice; accomplished literary writing sets the
craft ambition. Accurate coverage alone leaves the writing unfinished.

## New pieces and structural revisions

Use this composition process for a complete new narrative or a requested
substantial rewrite, including rejection of its argument, flow, interest or
voice. For a limited edit, preserve the existing construction and perform only
the requested change.

Read [Literary craft](references/literary-craft.md) before composing. It shows
how stance, sentence construction and information placement create an effect.
Use the control demonstrated there with the present author's words and facts.

Before drafting, separate the author's contention from the supporting record.
For an argument, work through what follows: what changes for somebody, what
assumption stops holding, what new difficulty or possibility appears? Develop
a particular insight beyond restating the brief's conclusion. For a personal
account, find the detail or event that changes how the experience is understood.
Use that thought to choose the material and its order. Keep the author's stated
position; develop its implications rather than substitute a different thesis.
A scene or example is one possible means of development, not a required slot.

Source constraints limit the claims you make; they are not automatically
sentences the reader needs. Include a qualification where the selected claim
would mislead without it. A personal hook can earn attention without explaining
the author's motivation. Respect the role the author assigned it.

Draft a complete piece around that thought. Revise its selection and order
before polishing sentences. Give each passage work that depends on what the
reader already understands. Keep qualifications beside the claims they limit.

### Finish with an independent reader

For a new piece or substantial rewrite, use a fresh agent as an editorial
reader. Give it the author's request, source record, current corrections,
complete draft and the literary-craft reference with its primary readings.
Keep your plan and self-assessment out of that handoff. Ask for a read of the
whole piece, not a rewrite or a list of preferred synonyms.

The reader must assess the author's particular perspective, development through
the body, voice, sentence movement and ending. Require passage-level reasons:
what becomes more interesting or consequential as the piece proceeds; where
it merely restates or adds another fact; whether the final emphasis follows
from the body. Check factual support and the author's instructions separately.
Accurate but inert prose requires revision. An entertaining invention does too.

Use the critique to revise at the level of the defect: an undeveloped thought
needs development, and an unrelated ending needs reselection or repositioning.
Sentence polish alone cannot resolve either. Send the complete revised piece
back for a read of the resulting whole. Finish after the reader finds the
piece's intended effect sustained through the ending and its claims supported.
A relative improvement over the previous draft is insufficient. Resolve a
reviewer's disputed factual or scope objection against the author's actual
sources; preserve explicit author decisions over a reviewer’s taste.

Deliver the finished piece; keep drafts, review and editorial analysis out of
the user's copy. For a limited edit, make and verify only the requested change.

## Preserve what the author means

For revisions, read the draft beside the corrections and identify the rejected
move. Recasting a resume inventory in livelier words still gives an inventory.
Preserve accepted improvements and meaningful distinctions. Before changing
accepted wording, name the problem the change solves and compare the meaning
side by side. A more professional tone alone supplies no reason to edit.
Research supplies understanding and choices; it does not dictate a paragraph
for each discovery.

Source remarks convey meaning, not necessarily publishable phrasing. "I care
about the result and happen to know the machinery" distinguishes interest from
ability. "I love the machinery" changes that interest; "results-driven and
technically skilled" erases the attitude. Preserve the distinction in the
public voice, rather than reproducing a private boast verbatim.

Check factual support separately from literary effect. Events, dialogue,
feelings, motives and causal links need source support. Figurative language
must express a real relationship. An invented incident cannot repair a weak
construction. A lively invention and a faithful but inert account both need
revision, for different reasons. Preserve deliberate ambiguity and emphasis;
a local copy edit does not authorize a new argument.
