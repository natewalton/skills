# Compose with stance, timing and inference

Read the continuous primary prose before the notes below. For an idea-led
post or essay, read [Chesterton's argumentative passage](primary-essay-passage.md).
For a personal account, profile or scene-led piece, read
[Wodehouse and Saki](primary-narrative-passages.md). For a piece combining these
forms, read both. The excerpts supply the writing itself; the short quotations
and commentary below explain selected choices rather than substitute for it.

These passages teach operations available to a contemporary nonfiction writer.
Borrow the control, not the authors' diction or their freedom to invent. The
speaker's supplied words establish the attitude; the factual record establishes
what happened.

## Let a judgment inhabit the sentence

In [Twain's “A Telephonic Conversation”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/142/pg142-images.html),
an interrupted writer observes:

> I notice that one can always write best when somebody is talking through a telephone close by.

He treats an obvious nuisance as an aid to concentration. The sentence gives
us the circumstance and his attitude together; an additional statement that
he finds interruptions annoying would repeat what the reader has
already understood. The one-sided call then occupies the page and develops that
irritation through experience.

Begin with what the author notices and their actual judgment of it. Let that
judgment determine the verb, comparison, or angle of the sentence instead of
adding an adjective about the experience afterward. The attitude may be amused,
exasperated, admiring, or serious. Irony works here because the reader can
recognize the reversal immediately. Use a direct observation where the reversal
would need explaining. A point of view can make ordinary material interesting
without an extra incident or a separate joke.

## Give sentence shape a job

Wodehouse opens [“Jeeves Takes Charge”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/65974/pg65974-images.html)
with Bertie defending his dependence on Jeeves:

> From the collar upward he stands alone

After an elaborate explanation of replacing the previous valet comes:

> They sent me Jeeves.

The collar makes praise of intelligence belong to this particular speaker's
world. The brief arrival gains weight from the winding explanation before it.
The contrast in length follows a change in narrative work: explaining the
predicament, then introducing the person who will alter it.

Find where the thought accumulates and where it turns. Carry connected setup
in clauses that belong together, then let the consequence arrive cleanly.
Put the revealing word where the sentence gives it emphasis. Several short
sentences in succession can make minor details sound equally momentous; several
long ones can bury the turn. Varying their lengths mechanically repairs neither
problem. Read the passage for the movement of attention and recast the sentences
around that movement. Keep the author's vocabulary rather than borrowing Bertie's.

## Give the reader something to discover

In [Saki's “The Open Window”](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/269/pg269-images.html),
Vera asks:

> Then you know practically nothing about my aunt?

What sounds like polite conversation establishes the visitor's ignorance.
The story she tells then makes ordinary details appear ominous. Those details
return before the final sentence changes our understanding of the storyteller:

> Romance at short notice was her speciality.

Compose from the reader's changing knowledge. Introduce a detail when it can
create a useful question; return to it when the reader can recognize a consequence
or a different significance. Explain what they need to follow the next movement,
leaving them the pleasure of making connections the material already supports.
A concluding moral would often repeat that discovery in weaker terms.

Nonfiction can control the order of true information. It cannot manufacture
Saki's events, conceal a qualification that changes a claim, or impose a twist
on material that has none. A plain consequence can be the arrival. Practical
instructions should put the necessary information where the reader can act.

## Let wit develop the argument

For an idea-led essay or opinion post, study how Chesterton argues in
[the opening essay of *All Things Considered*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11505/pg11505-images.html).
He objects to treating a philosophy's recency as evidence of its merit:

> he might as well call himself a Thursdayite.

The invented label exposes the irrelevance of the original label: a day of
the week supplies a date, not a reason to believe an argument. The joke does
intellectual work. His subsequent comparisons with fashionable bonnets and
sneering at a woman's age develop that same objection from different directions.
He has a particular contention, not a topic followed by some amusing asides.

For this form, identify the judgment the author wants a knowledgeable reader
to engage with. Find the concrete relationship that makes that judgment worth
considering. Develop the relationship in the author's way of seeing it: an
apt comparison, a consequence followed far enough to become interesting, or a
specific example whose implications accumulate. These are choices, not a
sequence to fill in. A personal joke at each end leaves an explanatory middle
unchanged. Let the stance influence the body as well as the hook.

Borrow this integration of thought and wit, not Chesterton's polemical persona
or a compulsory comic label. An analogy needs a real shared relationship;
an invented opposing view merely gives the writer something convenient to beat.
An original inference can be offered as the author's argument. Keep its premises
true and distinguish it from an observed result. An illustrative situation stays
illustrative after its frame is established; use that continuity to develop it
instead of repeatedly announcing its hypothetical status.
